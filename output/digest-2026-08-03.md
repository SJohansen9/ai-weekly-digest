### 🧠 Chain-of-thought forgery exposes fundamental LLM security weakness 🔴
*Source: MIT Technology Review | 30 July 2026*

| Stakeholder | Impact |
|---|---|
| 💼 **Business** | Organisations deploying LLMs in high-stakes workflows cannot rely solely on red-teaming and guardrail training. Researchers showed models identify instruction roles by writing style, not metadata tags, enabling spoofed internal reasoning to bypass safety controls. |
| 🌍 **Society** | As LLMs enter health, government, and consumer applications, a class of attacks that training alone cannot fully eliminate raises the stakes for human oversight and system-level isolation. |
| 🏛️ **Government** | Defence and public-sector adopters may need to reassess assumptions that frontier-model safety evaluations are sufficient before deployment in sensitive contexts. |

**Why it matters:** Researchers Jasmine Cui and Charles Ye presented findings at ICML this month arguing that chain-of-thought forgery — writing text that mimics a model's internal scratch-pad style — can trick LLMs into treating attacker text as their own reasoning. Tests against OpenAI, Anthropic, Alibaba, and DeepSeek models produced similar results. The team contends that because roles are foundational to how LLMs process instructions, perfect security may be unattainable through training alone.

**Likely responses:** Labs combine training with runtime monitoring, output filtering, and architectural isolation. Enterprises adopt zero-trust assumptions for agent workflows. Standards bodies may incorporate role-spoofing test cases into evaluation suites.
